# 01 — Vision and fiction

## Product promise

THE EYES turns being watched into a physical game rule. A survivor enters a room and asks: where is the Eye now, what can it actually see, what machinery lies in that direction, and which teammate can create a safe opportunity? The Eye asks the inverse questions: who is committing to an objective, which socket exposes that commitment, and is spending attention here worth losing sight of another route?

The antagonist is enormous, expressive, and immobile. It does not chase survivors down a corridor. It changes which parts of the architecture are dangerous. A player who learns the room should feel increasingly capable of manipulating it, even as the situation becomes more threatening. Fear comes from anticipation, compromised choices, unfamiliar machinery, and an intelligence looking for a mistake.

The original brief names survival horror, lethal social games, chaotic obstacle courses, and asymmetric multiplayer as inspirations. These are reference functions, not content to reproduce. THE EYES uses original characters, symbols, dialogue, mechanisms, spaces, and outcomes. Its distinctive combination is **fixed observation, gaze-authorized actions, persistent collective progress, and trials with recoverable mistakes**.

## Five pillars

1. **Observation has a cost.** The Eye cannot watch every room at once. Moving to another socket interrupts one line of sight and announces the new one. A hidden survivor is hidden from both human and AI control under the same rules.
2. **The rules are cruel but legible.** A trial explains its objective before punishment begins. A trap advertises its footprint and activation. Illusions create uncertainty about the world without lying about input prompts, accessibility settings, or verified completion.
3. **Cooperation changes the space.** Survivors create cover, divide information, rescue one another, and operate complementary machinery. Teamwork is an action with physical consequences, not only a score multiplier.
4. **Failure produces a new decision.** Early mistakes cost health, time, position, or a recoverable object. Punishment rooms return players to the match. Elimination is an accumulated consequence, not an arbitrary first-touch death.
5. **The nightmare remembers.** The environment responds to the team's choices through animation, sound, records, and late-match escalation. Permanent seals remain permanent. The fiction may be unreliable; the game state must remain trustworthy.

## Intended experience

A production Classic match targets approximately twelve to eighteen minutes. The first minute offers orientation, the middle produces contested trials, and the ending compresses distance into a dangerous escape. A good session gives survivors several readable successes even when they lose: a saved teammate, an outplayed gaze, a solved mechanism. A successful Eye should remember a prediction and a well-timed combination, rather than the ease of pressing a global damage button.

The target experience supports one player learning offline, a small group practicing, and a full asymmetric lobby. Higher player counts belong to separate variants because a room designed for six survivors does not automatically remain fair at sixteen. The camera, readability, traversal width, and objective contribution rules must be evaluated for each supported count.

## World: the institution of the Eyes

People disappear into **the Observance**, an institution that occupies the moment between waking and recognition. Its keepers do not ask whether a person is innocent. They ask whether a person can be made to accept a rule. Every realm resembles something ordinary that has been rebuilt by an intelligence that understands its function but misunderstands its comfort: a fairground without celebration, a home without belonging, a corridor without destination, a sky without distance.

The giant Eye is an aperture of that institution. Multiple sockets belong to one active consciousness during a match. The plural title describes the larger audience beyond the visible antagonist. Whether these other Eyes are judges, previous captives, a machine, or something older remains deliberately unresolved during the first release. Individual lore fragments may disagree about motive; they must agree about actions the player can observe.

The survivors are called **Witnesses** in the institution's language. Their assigned numbers are inventory marks, not their identities. They resist by retaining names, teaching one another, and leaving reliable instructions for the next group. The team's objective is to take three **seals of assent** from the machinery and break their function. A seal becomes an exit key only after the survivors have performed a task without accepting the institution's claim of ownership.

## Narrative structure

The First Observance begins in a receiving chamber. A voice describes the rules with excessive courtesy. The Eye opens, looks at a harmless object, and moves it. This establishes gaze as a verb before it becomes a threat. Survivors then discover that the voice omits useful information, while physical inscriptions left by former captives provide practical counters.

A full first season follows four acts corresponding to the four realms. Twisted Carnival tests the desire to perform. Labyrinth of Shadows tests certainty and authority. Dollhouse tests care, possession, and consent. Void of Despair tests memory and mutual recognition. Completing a realm changes the hub archive and adds alternative interpretations; it does not award combat power that disadvantages new players.

Each character has an arrival memory, three environmental conversations, a cooperative revelation, a private temptation, and two ending reactions. Ending reactions depend on rescue and escape events, not on opaque morality points. A selfish action may have a clear short-term benefit and a remembered social cost. The game avoids deciding that strangers who disagree have objectively bad character.

## Story delivery

Story is delivered while players can act. Plaques provide the immediate rule. Objects contain one-sentence inscriptions. Optional records take under twenty seconds to read or listen to and remain accessible in the archive. Longer conversations happen between observances. Mid-trial dialogue never obscures an urgent warning or consumes the only channel needed to identify a hazard.

The Eye speaks sparingly. It observes literal behavior and draws unsettling conclusions. Sample original lines: “You call that a hiding place because I permitted the name.” “The third seal is yours. The distance is still mine.” “Someone has held the door. How unlike this house.” These are voice direction examples, not required timing events in the current prototype.

The announcer is a distinct presence, **the Registrar**: calm, administrative, and incapable of saying that the institution has made a mistake. The Registrar explains actual game rules. The Eye may taunt but cannot falsify the Registrar's objective text. This division gives deception a fictional surface while protecting reliable communication.

## Tone and visual identity

The world combines monumental ritual architecture, worn industrial mechanisms, theatrical framing, and organic surveillance. Stone is cold, brass is tarnished, fabric absorbs light, and the Eye is wet and alive. The dominant palette is charcoal and desaturated blue, with amber for authority and pale cyan for a survivor-authored route. Red is reserved for an immediate hazard rather than used across every decorative surface.

Violence is consequential but does not require detailed anatomical spectacle. Machinery can be terrifying through scale, sound, anticipation, and aftermath. A player's defeat should be readable in a crowded online match and respectful of adjustable presentation settings. The production content rating is a planning decision to confirm after representative art and audio exist, not a rating claim made by this document.

Humor comes from character resilience, absurd bureaucracy, and physics mishaps that remain fair. It does not turn the central Eye into a gag or undermine a teammate's elimination. Cosmetics fit each character's silhouette and the realm's material language; novelty items that conceal hit states or socket direction are excluded.

## Non-negotiable fictional rules

The Eye cannot physically walk, fly freely, or appear in arbitrary locations. A socket transition is an event through established architecture. A gaze can trigger a prepared mechanism or authorize a permitted summon; it cannot invent a new room under a player without warning. Moving walls have occupied volumes and maintenance routes. Doors are anchored to real openings and have a destination on both sides.

Escaped survivors remain people in the story. The fiction may show that the wider institution persists, but the match's victory is never revoked by a surprise cinematic. A sequel hook must not tell a player that the rules they mastered were meaningless. The first production milestone is therefore a truthful complete observance before any larger season narrative.
